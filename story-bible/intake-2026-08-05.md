## 1. Tissadelle: empathy is what carries her command

Dermot's words, close to verbatim: *"Tissadelle's empathy makes her a popular
and capable leader if not a very confident one"* — and, clarifying the
confidence half: *"not over confidence or projecting a false harsh
confidence."*

Read together with the apparent-weakness paragraphs merged earlier the same
day (PR #329), the settled shape is:

- **The instrument of her leadership is empathy** — attention to what the work
  is costing the people doing it, acted on early. Popularity and capability
  both flow from that, not from command presence.
- **What she is *not* is a confidence-projector.** The two failure modes she
  declines are overconfidence and the performed-harshness that imitates it.
  This is the same fact as "she does not perform confidence she does not
  have," approached from the leadership side.
- The apparent-weakness framing stands: outsiders misread the absence of
  projection as self-doubt; the misreading never survives acquaintance.

Actioned same day: a Character Notes paragraph on her page (PR #331), scoped
exactly to the above. Consistent with the 28 July intake ("highly sensitive
but confident" — the sensitivity is now the named engine; the confidence
remains the calibrated kind, never the projected kind).

## 2. Ensemble principle: no gender stereotypes, strength and vulnerability are orthogonal

Dermot's words, close to verbatim: *"There are other strong female characters
and vulnerable male characters. No gender stereotypes as a whole. Some
characters are both strong and vulnerable at the same time."*

This is a **standing principle for the whole cast**, not a Tissadelle note —
it generalises the "this is Tissadelle, not a type" scope rule settled 28 July
(her particular pattern must not harden into "how this story writes women in
command"). Consequences for writing and review:

- Strength and vulnerability are **orthogonal to gender and to each other**.
  Neither is a gendered default anywhere in the cast, and possessing one says
  nothing about the other.
- The corpus already models this — treat these as the reference examples
  rather than inventing new archetypes: **Kate Ukrainka** (unsentimental
  professional strength *and* the self-diminishment flaw, simultaneously),
  **DI Albercombe** (the bureau's most reliable instrument *and* a man whose
  personal correspondence defeated him), **Sohrel** (three centuries of
  institutional nerve spent against one honest reopening), **Tissadelle**
  (capable command, no projection).
- Review test for new characters and chapters: if a trait assignment would
  read as a gender default — or if a character is only ever strong or only
  ever vulnerable — look again.

Not actioned as a page anywhere: this is authorial guidance. It should shape
drafts rather than be published as a statement.
