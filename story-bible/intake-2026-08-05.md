## 1. Tissadelle: empathy is what carries her command

Dermot's words, close to verbatim: *"Tissadelle's empathy makes her a popular
and capable leader if not a very confident one"* — and, clarifying the
confidence half: *"not over confidence or projecting a false harsh
confidence."*

Read together with the apparent-weakness paragraphs merged earlier the same
day (PR #329), the settled shape is:

- **The instrument of her leadership is empathy** — attention to what the work
  is costing the people doing it, acted on early. Popularity and capability
  both flow from that, not from command presence.
- **What she is *not* is a confidence-projector.** The two failure modes she
  declines are overconfidence and the performed-harshness that imitates it.
  This is the same fact as "she does not perform confidence she does not
  have," approached from the leadership side.
- The apparent-weakness framing stands: outsiders misread the absence of
  projection as self-doubt; the misreading never survives acquaintance.

Actioned same day: a Character Notes paragraph on her page (PR #331), scoped
exactly to the above. Consistent with the 28 July intake ("highly sensitive
but confident" — the sensitivity is now the named engine; the confidence
remains the calibrated kind, never the projected kind).

## 2. Ensemble principle: no gender stereotypes, strength and vulnerability are orthogonal

Dermot's words, close to verbatim: *"There are other strong female characters
and vulnerable male characters. No gender stereotypes as a whole. Some
characters are both strong and vulnerable at the same time."*

This is a **standing principle for the whole cast**, not a Tissadelle note —
it generalises the "this is Tissadelle, not a type" scope rule settled 28 July
(her particular pattern must not harden into "how this story writes women in
command"). Consequences for writing and review:

- Strength and vulnerability are **orthogonal to gender and to each other**.
  Neither is a gendered default anywhere in the cast, and possessing one says
  nothing about the other.
- The corpus already models this — treat these as the reference examples
  rather than inventing new archetypes: **Kate Ukrainka** (unsentimental
  professional strength *and* the self-diminishment flaw, simultaneously),
  **DI Albercombe** (the bureau's most reliable instrument *and* a man whose
  personal correspondence defeated him), **Sohrel** (three centuries of
  institutional nerve spent against one honest reopening), **Tissadelle**
  (capable command, no projection).
- Review test for new characters and chapters: if a trait assignment would
  read as a gender default — or if a character is only ever strong or only
  ever vulnerable — look again.

Not actioned as a page anywhere: this is authorial guidance. It should shape
drafts rather than be published as a statement.

## 3. Antagonists, not villains

Dermot's words, close to verbatim: *"there are antagonists and opponents,
using misguided or misaligned, but no pure villains."*

Standing principle for the whole cast, same register as §2. Every human-scale
(and person-scale) opposition in the story is **misguided or misaligned** —
wrong about something, optimising the wrong thing, loyal to an institution
past the point of examination — and never evil for its own sake. The corpus
already runs on this and the cosmology *enforces* it:

- The settled Layer 1/2 canon says evil is a shape collapse takes, not a
  founded thing — the Unfounded are patterns, catalogued but unaddressable,
  and explicitly **not persons**. So "pure villain" is not merely against the
  house style; in this cosmology it is a category error. A person always has
  grounding, and what has grounding can be misguided but not purely evil.
  The False Dragon codex entry is the doctrinal statement of the same rule.
- Existing reference examples of the antagonist-done-right register:
  **Maren Solveig Krast** (technocratic self-confidence at its least
  examined — misaligned, not malicious), the Rangers' commercial rivals in
  the Fellowship-of-light account (a competing case argued in good faith and
  answered by the record), Sohrel's Prime Ganglion office (institutional
  cost-accounting, not cruelty).
- Review test: if an opponent's motivation reduces to "because they are bad,"
  the draft is wrong — find what they are *for*, and let it be something a
  reasonable institution could have drifted into.

Like §2, this shapes drafts and review; it does not get a reader-facing page.

## 4. Direct violence is almost impossible to hide on a well-run station, ship or habitat

Dermot's words, close to verbatim. Unlike §2–3 this is a **world fact**, not
only a craft rule, and it quietly underwrites the whole shape of crime in this
canon: a sealed environment instruments everything — air handling, pressure
cycles, mass balance, door logs, med telemetry — so violence against a person
is the *least* concealable act available, and what survives as crime is what
the record itself can be wrong about. Paperwork crime. Registry drift.
Jurisdictional seams. The shrinking witness statement.

The corpus already behaves this way and now has its reason stated:

- Reeves confirmed the fifteenth door from *air-handling differentials and
  pressure-cycle telemetry* — the habitat's instruments notice a door, so they
  would certainly notice a body.
- The Warm Patch's crime scene "was the record" — four kilograms of flour
  could hide for eleven days only because it hid *in the books*, not in a
  corridor.
- The Five-O premise (matters habitats can't close alone) points at
  jurisdiction, not gore; the tone rule (unsettling, never horror) gets a
  structural justification for free.

Two boundary notes for future drafts: the qualifier **"well run" is
load-bearing** — a badly run or failing habitat is where that assumption
breaks, which is a *setting decision* to make deliberately, not a convenience
to reach for; and "almost impossible to **hide**" does not mean impossible to
*do* — it means the interesting question after violence is never whodunit but
what the institutions do with a record that already knows.

Candidate for promotion to a lore entry someday (it is a statable Archive-voice
fact); left here pending Dermot's call, per the standing rule that lore
asserts only what he has approved.

## 5. Most evil and suffering is dysfunctional rather than existential

Dermot's words, close to verbatim. This is the thematic capstone of §3 and §4:
evil and suffering in this canon are, in the main, products of things *running
badly* — institutions drifting past examination, records maintained wrong,
alignment lost, costs compounding unnoticed — and not features woven into
existence itself. The ground of being is not cruel; the machinery on top of it
malfunctions.

The settled cosmology already says the strong form of this and should be read
alongside it:

- Evil is **a shape collapse takes**, and the Unfounded are patterns with no
  origin condition — nothing underwrites them. Evil has no Layer 1. That *is*
  "not existential," stated as category fact: it is privation and parasitism,
  never a rival ground. (The old-Earth theological name for this position is
  the privation account, and the canon holds it without ever needing to cite
  it.)
- The Cosmic Limitation on Evil gives the mechanism: an entity in evil's
  service had only borrowed purpose, so when the purpose goes it stops being
  instantiated. Dysfunction ends when the function is repaired or withdrawn —
  which is exactly what "dysfunctional rather than existential" predicts.
- At human scale, the corpus's suffering is already dysfunction-shaped: a
  grandmother at half-entitlement because of a transfer code; a door
  maintained *out of* the plans; a hearing convened to review grievances that
  answered none. Nobody in any of those stories was cruel. Things ran badly,
  and people paid.

**"Most" is load-bearing.** The principle deliberately does not say *all* —
the remainder (whatever suffering is genuinely built into finitude: loss,
distance, the Ollune paying hourly for their own lift, a vacancy honestly
kept) stays available to the story, and the church-space overlay may read
that remainder devotionally without the main line ever depending on it.

Consequences for drafting: when suffering appears, its causal chain should
normally trace to something fixable-in-principle that failed — and the story's
consolations correspondingly run through *repair* (a corrected registry, a
recognised person, a working agreement) rather than through defeating a dark
power. A draft whose suffering has no dysfunction behind it is making an
existential claim, and those are Dermot's to make, one at a time.

## 6. True inner confidence is compatible with true empathy

Dermot's words, verbatim. This completes the Tissadelle model from §1 and
resolves the apparent tension inside it: what empathy excludes is the
*projected* kinds of confidence — overconfidence and performed harshness —
never confidence itself. Her calibrated inner certainty and her attention to
other people's costs are not rivals; the first funds the second. An officer
who needs the room to agree with her spends other people defending her
ground; an officer sure of her own ground can afford to give somebody else's
unshowable knowledge standing, and to put her own name on a delay.

Dramatised the same day in the S05E01C01 draft (*Refusal to Certify*): every
confident move available to command fails against Qiren Tal's refusal, not
because confidence is a vice but because confidence answers *am I right* and
the operative question was *what is this costing the one who knows*. Shepherd
solves it out of both qualities at once — the empathy to treat the refusal's
cost as evidence, and the inner certainty to stand alone on the result.

General form for the cast: strength/vulnerability (§2) and
confidence/empathy are the same kind of pair — orthogonal, combinable, and
most interesting when combined.
