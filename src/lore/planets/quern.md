Quern's weather report has not been revised since it was first filed, and the Survey Corps does not expect to revise it. The world has weather the way a river has a shape: violent everywhere, and the same violence in the same places, on no calendar at all, because Quern has no calendar to offer.

**Quern** is the innermost planet of the **Kiln System**, a red dwarf star small enough and close enough to hold its one rocky world face-on. The planet is tidally locked: its rotation and its orbit are the same twenty-nine hours, so one hemisphere keeps the star and the other keeps the dark, permanently. The first survey crew named the star for what it does and the planet for what the star does *to* it — a quern being the pair of millstones a hand-mill grinds between — and by the time they were done charting, the naming theme was carrying itself.

## The Standing Storm

The working term on the first survey was *the hurricane*, and the record keeps the word while noting everything wrong with it. Nothing in the storm is water. Nothing about it is tropical. And it will never move on or make landfall, having no coast, no season, and nowhere else to be.

Directly beneath the star, rock does not stay rock. The substellar region — the crews called it **the Glazing** — is an open melt, a desert of molten silicate under a sky that never changes its angle of light. Heat rising off the melt drives a single permanent cyclonic storm, anchored where its fuel is and organised into rotation by the planet's own twenty-nine-hour spin. What the storm carries is not water vapour but rock vapour: sodium and silicon monoxide boiled off the melt, lofted, cooled at the storm's rim, and returned as a thin, perpetual rain of stone. The storm cannot exhaust its energy source and cannot leave it, so it does what no storm on a turning world is permitted to do — it simply continues.

## The Night Side

The far hemisphere has never seen its sun. What warmth crosses to it arrives worn out, and the dark side pays the debt the bright side runs up: everything on Quern that can freeze ends there, laid down in order of its freezing points. Water ice nearest the light, in ranges pressed outward from the storm belt. Carbon dioxide frost beyond that, where water has long since finished falling. And at the antistellar deep — the coldest ground on the world, under a sky of unmoving stars — the air itself gives up. Nitrogen, the bulk of Quern's atmosphere, condenses and falls as snow.

The crews' name for those last fields was **the Meal**, that being what a quern produces, and the record has let it stand. It is the stillest place the Survey Corps has ever instrumented: no wind that a landed probe recorded above a whisper, no sound but the instrument's own, and snowfall too fine and too sparse to see except in a lamp beam — the atmosphere, arriving at its own funeral, a few grains at a time.

## The Millrace

Between the two hemispheres runs the belt the world is named for. Day-side air, hot and laden, pours toward the dark at altitude; night-side air, cold and dense, drains back toward the light along the ground. The two flows meet in a ring around the whole planet and do not mix so much as grind — a permanent shear zone, some few hundred kilometres wide, that the crews called **the Millrace** and the record calls the terminator belt when it is being formal.

It is the only place on Quern where liquid water exists, and the water is never still. Steam driven off the day side condenses the moment it crosses into the cold, and the winds it condenses into run along the belt, not across it, so the rain arrives horizontally — carried parallel to the ground at speeds the first survey logged and the second survey declined to fly in. The shear keeps supercooled droplets and ice in constant collision, and the charge that builds discharges where it builds: inside the rain. Lightning at the Millrace does not come down. It comes *along* — a lit seam travelling inside the weather, visible from orbit as a flicker running the ring of the world.

The belt is also, by a margin, the reason Quern has no standing surface instrumentation. Three proposals for a permanent meteorological station in the Millrace are on file, and each failed the same engineering review: there is no design on record that the belt would not eventually sand, drown, or strike, and a station that must be replaced is a survey schedule wearing a building's name. The long-baseline instruments stay in orbit.

## Balance or Collapse

One measurement gives Quern its standing scientific interest, and the record is careful about what the measurement does and does not say. Nitrogen is leaving the atmosphere. The snowfall at the Meal is deposition, and deposition is measurable: year on year, the antistellar cap gains, and the air is the only place the gain can come from.

Two readings of that fact are held on file. The first is equilibrium: ice under its own weight flows, however slowly, and the cap's slow spread back toward the light may return frozen atmosphere to the Millrace as fast as the dark lays it down — in which case the storms are a cycle, and Quern keeps its air indefinitely. The second is collapse: the return flow may not balance the fall, in which case the atmosphere is spending itself, the Millrace is the sound of it being spent, and Quern is partway through becoming an airless world with one bright molten eye — on a schedule of millions of years, which the world can afford and a survey baseline of decades cannot resolve.

The Archive holds both readings and has not been asked to choose between them; on the present data it could not, and says so. What the file notes instead is why the question is worth a standing entry at all: everywhere else in charted space, atmospheric collapse is read from its fossils — thin air over old ice, a finished event inferred backwards. Quern is the one catalogued world where the question of whether a world keeps its air can be *watched*, in progress, by anyone content to wait.

## Survey Status

Quern is uninhabited, has never been settled, and carries no proposal that it should be. It holds no charter and belongs to no polity; it is a catalogue entry with weather. Survey passes are periodic, orbital, and scheduled around nothing, since nothing on Quern is scheduled either. The one operational note the record carries forward from every pass is the first crew's, filed without elaboration and never improved on: *the world is not hostile; it is merely entirely occupied.*
