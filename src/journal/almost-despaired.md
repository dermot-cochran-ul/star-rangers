Three days ago I wrote about losing this book's name. This is the entry about finding the other one.

I want the record to say I *almost* despaired, and I want to be precise about the almost, because in the first telling I left it out. Finding a name that is unique and also evocative — genuinely both, not one wearing the other's clothes — spent most of a day looking impossible, and for a stretch in the middle I was ready to write that it was.

## Two different jobs

*Drithane* was never the problem. The title is doing its work: a spark, a drift, a word no character says. But a title and a setting's name are different jobs. A title competes on a shelf. A setting's name is the word for everything at once — the one you reach for when the book is only part of what you mean. Sanderson has *Cosmere*. I had "the site."

For a few hours on the third of August the browser tab said *Grand Ensemble Multiverse*, which is the Archive's own name for the whole of everything, and it was wrong in a way I could feel before I could argue it: it names the stage and not the watch. This work is as much about the Star Rangers as it is about the multiverse they stand in. A name that carries only the cosmology tells you the sky and forgets the people looking at it.

## The day of collisions

The hunt relearned every lesson of the retitle at higher speed, and added new ones.

The drift direction died in an afternoon. *Driftane* is an e-liquids and lighters brand, and separately an ambient-music channel. One letter over, *Driptane* is a medication with posted dosage information. Every road out of that phonetic neighbourhood that keeps the sound runs through somebody's shop or somebody's pharmacy.

Somewhere in the middle of this I checked the coined words already inside the book, and the results cured me of perfectionism permanently. *Kieme*, the second tier of the Cosmic Cascade, is the ordinary German noun for a fish's gill. *Levril* is an Italian intimate-hygiene gel. And there exists a fan wiki for someone else's fiction entirely — "The Fellowship of the Light of Christ" — whose invented universe independently contains a Levril, built by beings called the Frenar. I could not have invented that, and in a sense I didn't: the namespace of pronounceable coinages is simply full, and has been for years.

Which turned out to be the useful discovery. An in-universe word is met in context — a reader arrives at *Kieme* through the glossary, not through a search box, and no German reader is confused for more than a sentence. Only the front door has to win a search. Once I stopped demanding shelf-grade uniqueness from every word and demanded it only from the one over the door, the problem shrank to a single name. It just had to be a name that didn't exist, in a world where — as I wrote three days ago — any two ordinary English words are claimed and every fantasy-register coinage has been minted twice.

That is where the almost lives.

## The turn

The way out was the same door *Drithane* came through: stop coining and start compounding, in the language the Celtic Union carried off Earth.

*Fian* — the old word for the warrior bands, the Fianna: the ones you called when the thing that needed doing did not fit inside any one king's borders. *Ilchruinne* — the Irish word for the multiverse. The national terminology database marks it superseded; I looked at the replacement, judged it less elegant, and kept the old form — which felt like a very Irish decision to be making while naming a band of the Fianna.

**Fian-ilchruinne.** The multiverse Fianna.

It is never said in the story, and it never will be. No character speaks it, it is not in the glossary, and it is not going in — the same rule *Drithane* lives under, for the same reason: a name that stays outside the story gets to point at all of it. And this one points everywhere at once — at the corps, at the Celtic Union, at the Grand Ensemble the Archive keeps, at the Boirinn Uplands ancestry Tissadelle carries, at Tír na nÓg and Sí Gaoithe and the whole register this book has been borrowing from since its first page. It gestures and does not define, which is the entire trick.

It fails one test, and fails it completely: nobody who hears it aloud can spell it. I am at peace with that, because the tab is not the front door — the title is. *Drithane* remains the word you can say to a friend across a table. Fian-ilchruinne is the word over the door once you have already arrived. On the page it wears a display form, *Fian Ilchruinne*, two words and two capitals; in the tab it keeps its hyphen. I have opinions about this that nobody asked for.

## The part that rhymes

This book is several hundred thousand words about the difference between closing a file and keeping it open one more day. The Survey Archive does not file despair; it files open questions, and it holds them open past the point where everyone sensible has agreed to live with the discrepancy.

"Despaired" closes the file. *Almost* is the Archive's word. The file stayed open long enough for the answer to arrive — which, on the record, is how the answer usually arrives.
