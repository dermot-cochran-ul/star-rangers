[The previous entry](/star-rangers/journal/the-audience-keeps-the-books/) was about a story keeping its own books. This is the companion case: a story keeping somebody else's.

An adaptation does not open a new ledger. It borrows one, and the ledger comes with an audience attached — readers who arrive at the first episode already invested, already trusting, already extending the credit that an original series has to spend seasons earning. That feels like a gift, and on the launch spreadsheet it looks like one. It is actually a liability, in the strict accounting sense: it is owed back.

## Two debts, not one

Here is the complication that makes this worth an entry instead of a prosecution. I found the Netflix *Witcher* more coherent than the books, in some ways — and I don't think that is heresy. Sapkowski's saga grew outward from short stories; it is episodic by birth, its continuity is loose in places, and it trusts inference where a television season has to show its dates. An adaptation that tightens that is doing genuine work, and audiences barely punish it. Nobody demands the mess be preserved. Readers who love a baggy saga still accept — often quietly welcome — an adaptation that squares its chronology, merges its redundant minor characters, and builds connective tissue the source never bothered with. Fidelity-as-transcription was never the debt.

And yet the room emptied anyway. A show that could credibly claim tidier books than the books still lost the readers — which is the proof that there are two accounts here, not one. **Coherence** is the show keeping its own books: the previous entry's whole subject, audited episode against episode. **Custody** is the borrowed ledger: whose record is treated as *the* record. You can balance the first account immaculately while defaulting on the second, and the audience — who could not define either term if you stopped them on the street — will detect the default within a season.

## Stewardship and spending

Every departure from a source reads in one of two ways, and audiences are unnervingly good at telling them apart.

A departure reads as **stewardship** when it visibly serves what the record means — when the adapters are recognisable as readers. Cut a subplot for the season's shape, restructure a timeline, give a thin character the interiority the page only implied: all forgiven, often unnoticed, because the audience can feel the source being consulted underneath the change. The change argues *with* the record, in the record's own terms.

A departure reads as **spending** when the record's audience is being treated as an asset and the record's substance as an obstacle. The tell is usually a sentence in an interview rather than a scene: *it's our own thing now.* To a reader, that sentence lands exactly the way "you're remembering it wrong" lands on a viewer — the previous entry's worst register, with the ledger swapped. And it acts retroactively, the way broken trust always does: every earlier departure, including the good ones, gets re-read from interpretation into disposal. The tidying that was stewardship on Tuesday is asset-stripping by Friday, without a frame of the show having changed.

*The Witcher*'s particular misfortune is that the suspicion received a public confirmation. The lead actor was, very publicly, the saga's most devoted reader — the one person inside the production the book audience trusted as their proxy at the table. When he walked, the audience read his leaving as their own verdict delivered from inside. Whether that reading was fair is almost beside the point; departures have more causes than their coverage. What matters is that the reading was *available*, and by then it was the likeliest story on the evidence the audience held. Trust decides ambiguous cases — that is what trust is for — and the account was already overdrawn. Recasting the lead afterwards asked for fresh credit at the precise moment the old credit had failed, which is not a casting problem but a banking one.

One more thing the borrowed case clarifies: the break travels. The readers leave first, loudly. But a show that has spent its source has, from that point on, only its own books to keep — no inherited depth to draw against, no corpus quietly supplying the coherence underneath — and the show-only viewers, who never opened the books and owed them nothing, inherit the failure a season later by the previous entry's ordinary mechanism. Defaulting on the borrowed ledger is how the internal one starts to slip.

## A reading, not a correction

So what does the borrower actually owe? Not transcription — that's established. I think the answer is that an adaptation survives as a **reading**: one account of the record's events, by named hands, valid by their own lights. Audiences grant enormous latitude to a strong reading, including readings that reorder, compress, and flatly disagree with the source's emphasis. What they refuse is a **correction**: a version that behaves as if the record should now say what the adaptation says. "Here is our account of these events" and "this is what the story really was" can differ by nothing on screen and by everything in posture, and posture is what the audience audits.

This project stumbled into writing that rule down before I ever thought to apply it to television. The Codex exists precisely to hold strong readings — each entry is one named source's account, valid for its author, free to contradict the settled record and forbidden to overwrite it. And when I settled how this setting would treat approved derivative work by other hands, the whole arrangement reduced to one line: **canon binds it; it does not bind canon.** Every adaptation that keeps its audience behaves as if some version of that line were in force — the source remains the record, and the adaptation is a powerful account of it, superseded rather than vindicated where the two disagree. The ones that empty the room are the ones that pick up the canon pen with the other hand.

## The part that rhymes

The previous entry ended by saying that suspension of disbelief is the audience agreeing to keep watching while the story proves it believes itself. The borrowed case adds one clause. An adaptation must also prove it believes its *source* — not agrees with it everywhere, not copies it, but believes it the way the Codex's authors believe the record they are arguing with: as the thing their own account is an account *of*.

Nobody asks the borrower to return the ledger unchanged. They ask to be able to tell, from any page, whose ledger the borrower thinks it is.
