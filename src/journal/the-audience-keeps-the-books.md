Everyone has one: a series they did not so much stop watching as leave. Never at the dragon, or the faster-than-light drive, or the dead man walking out of the sea — the impossible thing was the premise, accepted in the first episode without a murmur. The leaving comes seasons later, at a moment that is, on paper, far less impossible than anything that came before it. And it has a particular shape: no announcement, no scene. One week you watch it the night it airs; then you notice you haven't; then you find you don't intend to, and hearing the title mentioned produces a small flinch of embarrassment, like a name from an old relationship.

"Suspension of disbelief" is a misleading phrase for what is actually going on. It suggests the viewer does it once at the door, like handing over a coat. Nothing is handed over. Disbelief is suspended, not abolished — held up by a live arrangement that gets renewed every episode, on evidence. It is credit, extended and re-extended, and the audience never stops being a creditor.

## What the audience is actually auditing

Not plausibility. Audiences forgive the impossible completely and the inconsistent never. A multiverse is one entry in the ledger, written on page one; the reader initials it and never raises it again. Everything audited after that is bookkeeping. Do the rules that constrained the hero in season one still constrain her in season five, or do they bend at exactly the moments the plot finds them inconvenient? Does a death stay died? When the established record and this week's episode disagree, which of the two does the show treat as wrong?

The moment a viewer catches the books being cooked, every earlier entry gets re-billed. The dragon only ever existed on credit, and the credit is what just failed.

## The relationship reading

A series that loses its audience this way has usually failed in one of two modes, and both of them have relationship names.

The first is **fake**. The story goes through the motions of feelings it no longer has: stakes announced in the trailer register, grief performed for a beat and then dropped, peril that everyone on both sides of the screen knows will cost nothing. You can tell the difference between a story that believes itself and a story performing belief the same way you can tell across a dinner table — not from any single line, but from an accumulation too distributed to quote. Nobody can name the episode where it went wrong. Everybody knows it did.

The second is worse: the story starts **spending** the audience's investment instead of earning its renewal. It contradicts their memory of what happened and implies they misremembered. It treats the years they have given it as leverage — *you've come six seasons, you'll swallow this*. And they do swallow it, for a while, longer than they should, which is what makes the eventual leaving so absolute. Trust of that kind does not fail back to neutral; it fails to contempt, and it fails retroactively. The ending reaches back and poisons seasons that were genuinely good when they aired. Nobody rewatches. The whole shelf is spoiled, the way the end of a bad relationship rewrites years that were, at the time, honestly happy.

## The covenant, written down

I keep discovering that this project's working rules are one rule wearing different clothes, and this is the angle that shows it. The rule is honesty with the record.

Every published chapter is canon — ratified last month, nothing provisional, so nothing can be quietly walked back later. A retcon here would be the Archive editing its own files, which is the one thing the Archive exists to not do. When two settled pages were found arguing with each other, the fix was not to pick a winner and erase the loser; the contested reading moved to the Codex with a named author attached to it — [filed, never erased](/star-rangers/journal/sorting-doctrine-out-of-lore/). The record is allowed to contain disagreement. It is not allowed to gaslight.

The consistency audit caught a chapter calling Threshold's clock discrepancy eleven years old in a year where the arithmetic makes it sixteen. A small thing; no reader would have written in. But it is exactly the class of error that reads as cooked books, and a story about an institution that refuses to pretend [forty seconds](/star-rangers/journal/two-copies-forty-seconds-apart/) aren't there cannot itself keep sloppy time. Even the URLs keep the covenant: when a chapter is renumbered, its comment thread and its citation address move with the content rather than silently reattaching to whatever now occupies the slot. A link is a small promise. The site will not tell you that you once commented on something you didn't.

None of this was designed from the analogy — each rule solved a local problem in its own week. Seen from here, they are one arrangement: the reader extends the credit, and everything on my side of the table exists so that the books balance on any day the reader chooses to audit them.

## The part that rhymes

The Survey Archive exists because somebody declined to close the file on a forty-second discrepancy. The audience is that somebody. They notice; they hold the file open longer than anyone sensible would; and how the story treats their discrepancy decides everything that follows. Acknowledge it, and they will follow the story anywhere — the impossible included. Tell them they imagined it, and they will leave without a scene, and they will not come back.

Suspension of disbelief is not the audience agreeing to believe. It is the audience agreeing to keep watching while the story proves it believes itself.
