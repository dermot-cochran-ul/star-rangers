::: pov bubochka

The second supply drop came down on a grey morning, and Bubochka went out to watch it because watching arrivals was on her exercise list. Crates, instrument cases, the delegation's mail. And one more thing, riding down the chain of hands the way cargo does — except it was not cargo, and nobody was carrying it so much as passing it along, each person glad to help with something somebody earlier had clearly approved.

She knew it before the last pair of hands. Small. Warm-looking. Exactly as easy to describe as it needed to be and not one detail easier.

Muffin.

Her training log already had a Muffin section. It recorded the weeks she had spent being worked by the galley door, and the week she had stopped being worked, and Barsik's one comment on that entry, which was *good; now log what it does next.* What it had done next, apparently, was find the one route off Eden that was shaped like a small adorable animal — the route her own paperwork had made. The manifest said the delegation travelled with a courtesy animal. Nobody on a loading dock counts courtesy animals. She understood, with a cold little drop of feeling she noted for the log, that her cover had a shadow, and the shadow had used it first. The word for that would come later. The fact went down now.

What she could not do was say any of this. *File, not volunteer* did not have an exception for feeling responsible, and she had checked. She could not hand a warden a hazard report from a rabbit, for the same load-bearing reasons Barsik could not hand one to a bureau from a cat.

But Barsik's other lesson was that a report is just a finding standing where the right reader already is. So Bubochka did the one thing her rules left her. When the last pair of hands set Muffin down in the yard, she went and sat where the warden could see them both at once — one small animal beside another, side by side, holding very still — and let the most accurate reader on the planet do the reading.

::: pov sorcha-nic-dhiarmada

Sorcha took delivery of the second drop the way she took delivery of everything: by the list. The list was a good one — the Survey people itemised their cargo down to the spare gasket — and the morning ran to it, crate for crate, until the chain of hands passed her something the list did not have.

It arrived the way well-vouched things arrive, which was the first wrong note: every hand along the chain had plainly assumed the hand before it knew. The clerk thought the quartermaster had cleared it. The quartermaster thought it was the mascot's — companion, he said, the word arriving a half-second late, like a man reaching for a rail that wasn't there. Nobody had brought it, in the sense that mattered. It had been *helped*, the whole way down, by people who would each have said they were only being kind.

She looked at it properly, because looking at animals properly was the trade.

Forty years of it, and the trade had never once come back empty. Sheep, dogs, hill hawks, the delegation's strange still rabbit with the working eyes — everything landed somewhere, told her what it wanted and what it would do about wanting it. This landed nowhere. Not the shape, which was fine, and was a rabbit, or was mostly, or had been a moment ago — she noticed her own account of it sliding and stopped trusting the account. The *asking* was wrong. Every animal she had ever fed had asked from inside some arrangement — earned, owed, bargained, begged. This asked the way rain gets into a boot: no arrangement at all, just the assumption of entry, worn smooth by never once having been refused.

Her mother's rule covered it. The rule had never needed to know what a thing was, only whether she could read it, and it had four words and took four seconds, and she said them in the yard, out loud, in front of the clerk and the quartermaster and the chain of helpful hands, in the tone she used for weather.

"Not from this house."

She set it down outside the gate herself, courteously, the way you'd return a parcel misdelivered. The quartermaster looked relieved in a way he could not have explained and did not try to. The clerk wrote something down. And the delegation's rabbit, she noticed, sat exactly where she had sat through the whole of it — beside the thing, still as a fence post, watching not the thing but *her*, with the unblinking professional attention of a colleague confirming that a job had been done right.

Sorcha had been noticing that rabbit for days. She revised the noticing upward, said nothing to anybody, and went back to the list.

::: pov sorcha-nic-dhiarmada

The thing did not leave the gate, and Sorcha had not expected it to. A creature that asked like never-refused would treat one refusal as weather, not law. It would wait for the house to change its mind, and in the meantime it would try the valley — and the valley was where her certainty ran out, because the valley was full of soft-hearted households and children with pockets, and she could not walk a dark-down against *this*. There was no custom for it. Yet.

What the valley had instead, it turned out, was the dogs.

Bran found it first, on the track by the lower field, and did what Bran did with anything standing where his map said nothing stood: dropped flat, fixed it with the eye, and moved it. No barking, no heat — the eye and the angle and the slow pressure of a dog whose whole cognition is the difference between where a thing is and where it should be. Muffin tried whatever it was Muffin did. Sorcha watched it happen and watched it *fail to land*, the way charm fails on a man reading a gauge. Bran wasn't refusing it. Refusal would have meant the question had been heard. Bran had no slot for the question. A collie does not ask whether you are charming. A collie asks where you should be, and the answer that came back — *not here* — was the only part Bran had any use for.

He put it at the gate. Meg, working the top field, put it at the gate an hour later, from the other direction. By evening the two of them had it filed under the only heading their trade possessed — livestock, errant, one — and were keeping it moved with the impersonal thoroughness they'd give a ewe with opinions. It was, Sorcha thought, the first time she had ever seen the thing *handled*. Not resisted, which fed it somehow — she could see that much, the way you see wind in grass. Handled. Placed. Made a fact of, by two professionals who had no idea it was anything else and were right anyway.

The children came, of course, with pockets. She gave them the house rule, and the reason her mother had given her — *what we can't read, we don't feed* — and watched the rule do what rules did in this valley: not get enforced. Get kept. Within three days the households along the chain had it, the way they had the dark-down times, passed kitchen to kitchen without anyone announcing anything. The thing sat at gate after gate, being asked the collies' question and given the valley's answer, and if it was learning anything from either, that was between it and whatever it kept accounts with.

She asked the clerk, once, directly: what is it. He said, carefully, that it was on no manifest. Then it can be on no doorstep, she said, and they both wrote that down in their separate kept ways.

::: pov bubochka

Bubochka's report on the whole affair ran to a page and a half, which was long for her, and she rewrote it twice to get the order right. What happened, then what worked, then what she thought — Barsik was strict about keeping the last part last and shortest.

What worked came in two kinds, and the difference between them was the finding.

The dogs were the first kind. Muffin's whole method was a question — *wouldn't it be easier to say yes?* — and the dogs simply did not have anywhere to put the question. Like Thrum, she wrote, then crossed it out, because it was not like Thrum. Thrum could not hear the question. The dogs heard everything and had no *use* for it. They were not resisting Muffin. They were working, and Muffin was a sheep in the wrong field, and that was the entire conversation. You cannot wear down someone who is not holding anything against you. You are just weather to them, and they are a wall.

The warden was the second kind, and the harder one to write up, because the warden did the exact thing Barsik said Eden could not arrange. She refused it. Out loud. In front of people. First try, four seconds, no apology — and then the valley copied her, not because she told them to but because copying kept things was what the valley *did*. Bubochka thought about the crossing night, all those houses turning their own lights out with nobody making them, and wrote her one sentence of what-she-thought: *this valley practises saying no to itself, kindly, on a schedule, and so nobody here is out of practice when something else asks.*

Filed. Not volunteered.

The delegation leaves after the next crossing, and the shuttle manifest has a line on it shaped like a small animal, and nobody has decided anything yet. Bubochka has logged the question and both possible endings, and has begun, quietly, sitting where the quartermaster can see her at loading times. It is not volunteering. It is just standing where the right reader is.
